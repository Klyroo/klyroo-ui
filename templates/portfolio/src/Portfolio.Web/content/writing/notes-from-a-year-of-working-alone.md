I left a team of nine to work on my own a year ago. These are the notes I would have wanted at the start, in the order I learned them, mostly by getting them wrong first.

## Saying no is the main skill

In the first three months I said yes to everything, and I did mediocre work for people whose problems I did not find interesting. It was not the money that changed it. It was a week in which three projects needed me at once, and I could not do any of them well.

I now ask three questions before I take a project: do I understand what success looks like for them, can I name the person who decides, and will I still be glad to work on it in the fourth week? Two yeses and a maybe is a no.

## One project at a time, mostly

I can do two things at once when one of them is small and the other has a clear edge. Otherwise I take one, and I tell the others when I will be free. Clients accept "February" far more often than I expected, and they accept it better than a rushed March.

## A calendar I can trust

I block the mornings for the work that needs thinking, and I keep meetings for the afternoons. It sounds trivial, and it is the one habit that changed my output. A calendar I cannot trust is a list of wishes.

## Write things down for the client

Working alone means nobody remembers a decision but you and the person who made it. I write a short note after every call: what we decided, what is open, who owns what. It prevents more arguments than any contract.

::: note
I use the same note for the next call's agenda. It means the second meeting starts where the first stopped, and the clients seem to like that more than the work.
:::

## What I miss

I miss the colleague who says "that is not what I would do" in a code review, because it is the best way to find out what you assume. I have replaced it with a small group of other independent people who read each other's work once a month. It is not the same, and it is enough.

## What I would tell myself

Charge more, earlier. Take longer holidays. And write the case study while the project is fresh, because in six months you will have forgotten why you made half of the decisions.
